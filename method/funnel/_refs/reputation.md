# reputation

Read a company before committing a person's time to it. The cost of skipping this is a call that should never have been scheduled, and the person pays it in the one resource the search actually runs out of.

## The four questions

None of them is answered by the page describing who the company is.

1. **What problem do they solve, and for whom.** Stated the way a customer would state it, not the way the marketing page does. When this cannot be answered in two sentences after fifteen minutes of reading, that is itself a finding.
2. **What changed in their world in the last thirty days.** A funding round, a launch, a shutdown, a competitor's move, a regulation. This is what makes an answer to "why us" sound like somebody who has been paying attention.
3. **Who they compete with, and what separates them.** The person will be asked some version of this, and the honest answer is more interesting than the flattering one.
4. **What the culture is actually like.** Not the values page. What people who left say, and how many of them say the same thing.

## The pattern rule

One negative review is noise. Somebody was fired, somebody had a bad manager, somebody is writing on their worst day. Read it and move on.

Seven to nine independent former employees with short tenure saying the same thing about leadership, product integrity or turnover is a pattern, and a pattern is a finding. Independent means different roles, different teams and different dates, because ten reviews from one layoff week is one event.

Treat a convergent pattern as disqualifying rather than as something to weigh against the compensation. Say so before any time is spent on a call, and say it plainly.

On 2026-08-16 a screening call was cancelled the day before it happened, with the best compensation in the whole funnel on the table, on exactly this evidence. That is the standard. A pattern that holds across independent sources is the most reliable prediction of the next two years available before joining, and the compensation on the table is the thing most likely to argue against reading it honestly.

## Where the reviews live

The reviews and salary site blocks automated clients, so it gets the same treatment as tier two of `method/funnel/_refs/sources.md`. Hand the person the exact path and the exact thing to copy.

On Glassdoor, the path is the company page, then the `Reviews` tab, then filter `Employment status` to `Former` and sort by `Most recent`. Quote the labels, because the site renames things and a person hunting for a control that is called something else gives up.

```
Open the company page and go to the reviews tab. Filter to former
employees, sort by most recent, and read the first two pages. Paste back
the cons column only, plus the job title and the tenure on each one.
Skip the pros. Fifteen to twenty rows is enough to see whether there is
a pattern or just noise.
```

Ask for the cons and the tenure because that is what the pattern rule reads. Asking for whole reviews gets a smaller sample pasted and takes longer to produce.

## Reading the public profile of a person in the room

Only public information, and only what they chose to publish. No inference about their background, their politics or their situation, and no speculation about why they moved jobs.

A fact about a person becomes a question to ask them, never a line recited back at them. Somebody who spent four years on the infrastructure side gets asked how the platform team and product split ownership today. The same fact delivered as "I saw you spent four years on infrastructure" lands as surveillance, and the room cools.

What goes in the dossier is the question, written out. What never goes in is the fact on its own, because a fact with no question attached gets recited by a person under pressure who is looking for something to say.
