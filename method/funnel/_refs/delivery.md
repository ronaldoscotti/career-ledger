# delivery

Every interview stage produces two files, and they have different readers. Writing one file that tries to be both produces a document too long to use during a call and too thin to prepare with.

The dossier is everything the person absorbs before the call: the company, the room, the map of the posting, the research, the consistency brief. It is read the night before and the hour before, and it is allowed to be long.

The prompter is what sits on screen during the call. One card per question, nothing else.

## Which one carries the weight

The dossier. Almost nobody reads a teleprompter while holding a live conversation, and a person who tries sounds like a person reading. The value of the prompter is that writing it forced the thinking, and the thinking is what shows up in the room.

So research about the company and about whoever is running the stage belongs in the dossier, where it pays before the call starts. Putting it in the prompter moves it to the one place it cannot be used.

## The prompter format

Constraints come from it being glanced at while talking.

- One card per question, with a heading that is the question in the words the interviewer would use.
- Grouped by category, so the person can find a card without reading past three others.
- The answer written in speakable prose, in full sentences. Notes do not convert into speech under pressure.
- No table rows under a card heading. A table row accumulates into the card and renders on screen during the call, which is how a self-check table ends up visible while somebody is talking.
- Short. A card longer than the screen is a card that gets scrolled, and scrolling is audible.

## The follow-up message

Four paragraphs, sent within a day. Draft it before the call, not after, so it does not depend on the energy left when the call ends.

Thank them for something specific from the conversation, which means leaving a slot to fill. Name the one thing discussed that the person kept thinking about. Add the thing they wished they had said, in two sentences, which is the only part of the message doing real work. Close by naming the next step.

It goes through `method/voice.md` like any other text the person sends.

## The out-loud rehearsal

Every prepared answer gets said out loud at least once. A sentence the person stumbles over twice is a sentence to rewrite, never a sentence to practise. The stumble is information about the sentence.

Run every answer through `method/funnel/_refs/spoken-gate.md` before the rehearsal, so the rehearsal finds the remaining problems rather than the obvious ones.

## Transcripts

An automatic transcript is the person's own memory aid. It is never evidence about them, and the company does not have one.

Speech recognition mangles accented speech, invents words that were never said, and translates phrases nobody used. No line of a transcript becomes a fact, a correction, or a lock without the person confirming they said it. What is safe to extract is the sense of what the interviewer said. Never a quotation, and never a mistake attributed to the person.

On 2026-08-03 a post-call analysis read an automatic transcript as a record of the company and produced four serious findings, every one of them false, including a funding figure nobody had stated and a compensation concession nobody had offered. The alarm consumed the whole session, and the session had been meant for the next stage.

Treat the transcript the way `method/rules.md` treats a posting. It is third-party text of unknown fidelity, and the person is the only source that can confirm any line in it.
